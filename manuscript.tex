\begin{abstract}
Low synchronous inertia increases the need for fast active power reserves that operate without centralized dispatch. Data center uninterruptible power supply (UPS) fleets contain multi-megawatt battery reserves that remain idle during normal grid operation. We introduce PULSE (Primary frequency UPS Local Subsecond Event triggered control), a decentralized controller combining local derivative droop control with an adaptive, time relaxing event threshold. Under ambient frequency drift, PULSE transmits zero standby messages; during a contingency, it reduces messaging by $63.6\%$ relative to fixed threshold control. In a 300~MVA low inertia benchmark, 200 agents with $12$~MW aggregate capacity limit the frequency nadir to $59.721$~Hz after a $5\%$ generation trip. The fleet adds $41.7\%$ virtual inertia and reduces the 500~ms sliding window rate of change of frequency from $-0.441$~Hz/s to $-0.285$~Hz/s. With phase locked loop and synchrophasor measurement filtering, aggregate power reaches $90\%$ in $65.0$~ms without actuator saturation or inverter chatter. Frequency domain analysis establishes a delay margin of $1.58$~s in the idealized regime and $0.952$~s under nominal filtering. A 50-run Monte Carlo campaign yields a nadir of $59.592 \pm 0.0002$~Hz at 95\% confidence. A 30~s support event uses $0.166$~kWh per rack and leaves more than 40~min of autonomous server backup runtime, meeting modeled data center reliability requirements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Displacing synchronous thermal generation with inverter-based renewable generation reduces rotational kinetic energy in power networks \cite{tielens2016,ulbig2014}. Following generation trips or intertie trips, low inertia grids exhibit steep initial rates of change of frequency (RoCoF) and low frequency nadirs. When mechanical turbine governors respond too slowly, underfrequency load shedding (UFLS) relays trip and risk cascading failures \cite{dulal2026inertia,beck2007}. Transmission system operators monitor system inertia and recruit fast active power reserves to limit frequency excursions \cite{dulal2026inertia,xu2014}.

Grid interconnection codes specify fast frequency response (FFR) and synthetic inertia from inverter-interfaced battery energy storage systems (BESS) \cite{he2026coordinated,abdollahi2026flexibility,erickson2020ffr}. Constructing dedicated utility scale battery facilities requires dedicated land and capital expenditure. Data center infrastructure offers an alternative storage resource: hyperscale facilities house multi-megawatt electrochemical uninterruptible power supply (UPS) fleets, installed for $N+1$ or $2N$ hardware redundancy \cite{wierman2014,mohammadi2026giups}. These electrochemical assets remain float-charged and idle during normal utility operation, discharging only during substation outages until on-site diesel generators synchronize \cite{yu2025reliability}. Survey studies summarize grid services that can tap this reserve capacity \cite{mohammadi2026giups}, and experimental facilities confirm that compute workloads and power feeds adjust to grid dispatch signals \cite{colangelo2026gridinteractive}.

Deploying data center UPS batteries for synthetic inertia and primary frequency regulation involves three specific technical challenges:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Decentralized Execution and Communication Load:} Centralized dispatchers polling hundreds of rack level inverters over industrial Ethernet or SCADA links incur transmission latencies and present single points of failure \cite{boyle2024aggregator}. Periodic polling consumes continuous channel capacity. Fixed threshold event triggered control (FETC) eliminates standby messages but emits redundant update bursts during transient frequency swings \cite{tabuada2007,zhuang2025dses}.
    \item \textbf{Sensor Dynamics and Noise Filtering:} Several formulations assume instantaneous RoCoF calculations, reporting sub-cycle responses below $10$~ms \cite{dimarogonas2012}. Practical inverter phase locked loops (PLLs) and IEEE C37.118 phasor measurement units (PMUs) require measurement windows between $16.7$~ms and $100$~ms to filter harmonics and noise \cite{ieeec37118}. Neglecting these filters conceals derivative chatter and actuator saturation.
    \item \textbf{Data Center Backup Runtime Guarantees:} Facility operators maintain IT equipment uptime above $99.999\%$ \cite{yu2025reliability}. Grid support routines must preserve autonomous runtime thresholds (such as Uptime Institute Tier III and Tier IV criteria), enforce minimum state of charge (SoC) reserve floors, and limit battery cell degradation.
\end{enumerate}

Prior literature examines distributed storage coordination at distinct control levels. Kang et al.~\cite{kang2024hess} formulated event triggered consensus for microgrid hybrid storage under denial of service conditions, focusing on secondary regulation over multi-second horizons. Zhuang and Fang~\cite{zhuang2025dses} developed adaptive event triggered scheduling for shared batteries on the IEEE 14-bus network during economic dispatch intervals. Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2025epsr} developed hierarchical event triggered control for DC microgrids. Tao and Gadh~\cite{tao2025coordinated} coupled electric vehicle fleets and data center storage through centralized supervisors. Boyle et al.~\cite{boyle2024aggregator} analyzed aggregation architectures for wind storage plants; Abudyak et al.~\cite{abudyak2026gfm} modeled grid forming data center inverters for intentional islanding transitions.

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} contrasts PULSE with these existing frameworks. Earlier formulations target secondary dispatch intervals, rely on unfiltered frequency derivatives, omit closed-loop delay margin derivations, or aggregate storage without rack level reserve protection. PULSE integrates subsecond local actuation, filtered frequency sensing, and local reserve floors. This work addresses the following topics in \textit{Sustainable Energy, Grids and Networks}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Control and planning of centralized and distributed energy storage}: Decentralized allocation of fast power reserves across multi-agent battery arrays while respecting local state of charge boundaries.
    \item \textit{ICT integration in electrical and energy systems for monitoring and control}: Communication aware event triggering over local networks subject to packet drops and latency jitter.
    \item \textit{Modeling of cyber-physical energy and communication systems}: Coupled frequency domain and time domain stability models linking grid swing mechanics with discrete communication protocols.
    \item \textit{Demand side management and demand response protocols}: Using installed data center infrastructure for clean grid stabilization in support of UN Sustainable Development Goals 7 and 11.
\end{itemize}
We designate this architecture \textbf{PULSE} (\textbf{P}rimary frequency \textbf{U}PS \textbf{L}ocal \textbf{S}ubsecond \textbf{E}vent triggered control). It controls distributed rack level UPS inverters using local frequency measurements and adaptive thresholds.

The main technical contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Decentralized Adaptive Event Triggered Architecture:} We formulate PULSE with an autonomous, time relaxing local event threshold. This mechanism eliminates transmissions during ambient frequency drift and prunes post-contingency message counts by $63.6\%$ relative to fixed threshold control over a 30~s window.
    \item \textbf{Analytical Virtual Inertia and Realistic Sensing Dynamics:} We derive closed-form expressions for virtual inertia ($H_v = 1.333$~s) and virtual damping ($D_v = 6.0$~pu) supplied by the 200-unit fleet. While an idealized unfiltered benchmark reaches $90\%$ power injection in $7.3$~ms, realistic 1-cycle PLL and 2-cycle PMU filters produce rise times of $65.0$~ms and $90.5$~ms, suppressing derivative chatter.
    \item \textbf{Frequency Domain Stability and Delay Margin Derivations:} We derive open-loop and closed-loop transfer functions for coupled swing and battery dynamics. Under nominal gains, the gain crossover frequency is $\omega_c = 1.25$~rad/s with phase margin $68.2^\circ$, producing a delay margin of $0.952$~s under nominal filtering and $1.58$~s in the idealized regime.
    \item \textbf{Battery Autonomy, Degradation, and Monte Carlo Evaluation:} Enforcing an $80\%$ SoC reserve floor restricts a 30~s frequency event to $0.166$~kWh per rack ($33.1$~kWh fleet total). Mean autonomous IT backup runtime remains at $46.8$~min ($40.0$~min minimum), satisfying Tier III and Tier IV criteria with $100\%$ compliance. A 50-seed Monte Carlo sweep under stochastic latencies, $30\%$ packet drop rates, and sensor noise yields a frequency nadir of $59.592 \pm 0.0002$~Hz.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methods and System Modeling}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Substation System Frequency Response (SFR) Formulation}
\label{sec:sfr}

We model power grid frequency dynamics following active power imbalances using an aggregated low order system frequency response (SFR) structure \cite{anderson1990}. The substation model couples rotational swing mechanics with a reheat turbine governor loop:
\begin{align}
2H\,\frac{d\Delta f(t)}{dt} &= \Delta P_m(t) - \Delta P_L(t) - D\,\Delta f(t) + \frac{P_{\mathrm{bess}}(t)}{S_{\mathrm{base}}}, \label{eq:swing}\\
T_g\,\frac{d\Delta P_m(t)}{dt} &= -\Delta P_m(t) - \frac{\Delta f(t)}{R}, \label{eq:governor}
\end{align}
where $\Delta f(t)$ is per-unit system frequency deviation from nominal frequency $f_0 = 60.0$~Hz, $H$ is the system synchronous inertia constant ($H=3.2$~s), $D$ is the load damping coefficient ($D=1.0$~pu), $R$ is governor speed droop ($R=0.05$~pu, corresponding to gain $1/R = 20.0$~pu), $T_g$ is the turbine governor time constant ($T_g=6.0$~s), and $\Delta P_L(t)$ is the step disturbance in per-unit on substation base capacity $S_{\mathrm{base}} = 300.0$~MVA. The nominal contingency is a $5\%$ generation drop ($\Delta P_L = 15.0$~MW, $0.05$~pu). Aggregate power injected by $N=200$ rack level UPS units is $P_{\mathrm{bess}}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^N p_i(t)$ in MW. Table~\ref{tab:params} details all simulation parameters.

\subsection{Analytical Virtual Inertia and RoCoF Formulation}
\label{sec:inertia}

Each UPS agent $i$ determines an internal candidate power command $p_i^\ast(t)$ from delayed local frequency feedback:
\begin{equation}
p_i^\ast(t) = \mathrm{sat}\!\left(-K_d\,\dot{\widehat{\Delta f}}_i(t) - K_p\,\widehat{\Delta f}_i(t),\ [0,\, P_{\max,i}]\right),
\label{eq:pstar}
\end{equation}
where $\widehat{\Delta f}_i(t)$ and $\dot{\widehat{\Delta f}}_i(t)$ are filtered frequency deviation and RoCoF measurements, $K_d$ is the derivative gain, $K_p$ is proportional droop gain, and $P_{\max,i}$ is available discharge power. The lower saturation boundary is zero, restricting UPS units to upward active power discharge during underfrequency events.

In the linear operating regime prior to inverter saturation or governor action, aggregate injection is $P_{\mathrm{bess}}(t) \approx -N K_d \dot{\Delta f}(t) - N K_p \Delta f(t)$. Substituting this term into the substation swing equation (\ref{eq:swing}) yields:
\begin{equation}
2H\,\dot{\Delta f}(t) = \Delta P_m(t) - \Delta P_L(t) - D\,\Delta f(t) - \frac{N K_d}{S_{\mathrm{base}}}\,\dot{\Delta f}(t) - \frac{N K_p}{S_{\mathrm{base}}}\,\Delta f(t).
\label{eq:swing_expanded}
\end{equation}
Grouping acceleration derivatives $\dot{\Delta f}(t)$ and damping terms $\Delta f(t)$ reveals equivalent closed loop swing dynamics:
\begin{equation}
2\left(H + H_v\right)\dot{\Delta f}(t) = \Delta P_m(t) - \Delta P_L(t) - \left(D + D_v\right)\Delta f(t),
\label{eq:effective_swing}
\end{equation}
where virtual inertia constant $H_v$ and virtual damping coefficient $D_v$ from the $N=200$ unit fleet evaluate to:
\begin{equation}
H_v = \frac{N \cdot K_d}{2 \cdot S_{\mathrm{base}}}, \qquad D_v = \frac{N \cdot K_p}{S_{\mathrm{base}}}.
\label{eq:hv_dv}
\end{equation}
Evaluating with nominal linear parameters ($N=200$, $K_d = 4.0$~MW$\cdot$s, $K_p = 9.0$~MW/pu, $S_{\mathrm{base}} = 300.0$~MVA):
\begin{align}
H_v &= \frac{200 \times 4.0}{2 \times 300.0} = \frac{800.0}{600.0} = 1.3333\,\mathrm{s}, \label{eq:hv_val}\\
D_v &= \frac{200 \times 9.0}{300.0} = \frac{1800.0}{300.0} = 6.0000\,\mathrm{pu}. \label{eq:dv_val}
\end{align}
The UPS fleet increases effective system inertia from $H = 3.20$~s to $H_{\mathrm{eff}} = H + H_v = 4.5333$~s ($+41.67\%$) and expands total damping from $D = 1.0$~pu to $D_{\mathrm{eff}} = D + D_v = 7.00$~pu (a sevenfold increase).

The theoretical initial RoCoF at contingency onset $t = t_{\mathrm{fault}}^+$ before governor intervention ($\Delta P_m(0^+) = 0$, $\Delta f(0^+) = 0$) follows from Eq.~(\ref{eq:effective_swing}):
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{df}{dt}\right|_{t=0^+} = -\frac{\Delta P_L}{2\,H_{\mathrm{eff}}\,S_{\mathrm{base}}} \cdot f_0.
\label{eq:rocof_theoretical}
\end{equation}
Without battery support ($H_{\mathrm{eff}} = 3.20$~s), a $15.0$~MW ($0.05$~pu) generation trip yields initial RoCoF:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RoCoF}_0^{\mathrm{base}} = -\frac{0.05}{2 \times 3.20} \times 60.0 = -0.4688\,\mathrm{Hz/s}.
\label{eq:rocof_base_calc}
\end{equation}
With PULSE synthetic inertia active ($H_{\mathrm{eff}} = 4.5333$~s), the theoretical initial RoCoF is:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RoCoF}_0^{\mathrm{pulse}} = -\frac{0.05}{2 \times 4.5333} \times 60.0 = -0.3309\,\mathrm{Hz/s},
\label{eq:rocof_pulse_calc}
\end{equation}
reflecting a $29.4\%$ reduction in initial frequency decay rate. In practice, RoCoF is evaluated over rolling finite difference windows of length $T_w$ \cite{dulal2026inertia}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RoCoF}(t;\, T_w) = \frac{f(t) - f(t - T_w)}{T_w}.
\label{eq:rocof_window}
\end{equation}
We evaluate RoCoF over two industry standard windows: a fast $T_w = 100$~ms window capturing initial inertial trajectory, and a standard $T_w = 500$~ms window matching ENTSO-E and IEEE generator withstand specifications.

\subsection{PULSE Adaptive Event Triggering Architecture}
\label{sec:thresholds}

Each UPS agent applies a local event triggering rule to suppress steady state message traffic and prevent actuator chatter \cite{tabuada2007,dimarogonas2012}. Agent $i$ updates the active power setpoint sent to its local inverter when:
\begin{equation}
\left|p_i^\ast(t) - p_i(t^-)\right| > \eta_i(t)\,P_{\max} \quad \text{and} \quad t - t_i^{\mathrm{last}} \ge \delta,
\label{eq:trigger}
\end{equation}
where $p_i(t^-)$ is the active power setpoint stored in the inverter zero order hold (ZOH), $\delta = 1.5$~ms is an anti-Zeno refractory dwell time, and $\eta_i(t)$ is the event threshold.

In baseline fixed threshold event triggered control (FETC), $\eta_i(t) \equiv \eta_0 = 0.02$. Although FETC eliminates standby communication, the constant threshold causes repeated triggering during settling oscillations. In PULSE, each agent relaxes its threshold autonomously following the detected onset of a grid contingency ($t \ge t_i^{\mathrm{onset}} = t_{\mathrm{fault}} + \tau_i$):
\begin{equation}
\eta_i(t) = \eta_0 + (\eta_{\mathrm{r}} - \eta_0)\left(1 - \exp\left(-\frac{t - t_i^{\mathrm{onset}}}{\tau_{\mathrm{r}}}\right)\right),
\label{eq:adaptive}
\end{equation}
where $\eta_0 = 0.02$ maintains sensitivity during the initial subsecond transient, $\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = 0.09$ relaxes the threshold as the governor stabilizes the system, and $\tau_{\mathrm{r}} = 0.60$~s controls the relaxation rate. This transition runs locally without supervisor communication.

\subsubsection{Theoretical Bound on Tracking Error and Nadir Degradation}
\label{sec:theoretical_bound}
The interaction between the discrete event triggered zero order hold and continuous swing dynamics produces an actuation discretization error $e_i(t) = p_i^\ast(t) - p_i(t)$. By design of Eq.~(\ref{eq:trigger}), $|e_i(t)| \le \eta_i(t) P_{\max}$ between trigger instants. When message packets encounter latency $\tau_i$ and Bernoulli packet drop probability $p_{\mathrm{drop}}$, the maximum setpoint error across agent $i$ is bounded:
\begin{equation}
\sup_{t \ge 0} |e_i(t)| \le \eta_{\mathrm{r}} P_{\max} + L_p (\tau_i + k_{\mathrm{drop}} T_{\mathrm{retry}}),
\label{eq:error_bound}
\end{equation}
where $L_p = \sup_t |\dot{p}_i^\ast(t)|$ is the Lipschitz continuity constant of the local candidate power command, $k_{\mathrm{drop}}$ is the consecutive packet drop count, and $T_{\mathrm{retry}}$ is the effective update interval. Summing over $N$ storage units, aggregate power deviation satisfies:
\begin{equation}
|\Delta P_{\mathrm{bess}}(t) - P_{\mathrm{ideal}}(t)| \le N \eta_{\mathrm{r}} P_{\max} + N L_p (\bar{\tau} + p_{\mathrm{drop}} \Delta t_{\mathrm{eff}}).
\label{eq:fleet_bound}
\end{equation}
Substituting this bound into swing equation (\ref{eq:swing}) yields an analytical upper bound on maximum frequency nadir degradation $\Delta f_{\mathrm{err}}$ relative to continuous droop:
\begin{equation}
|\Delta f_{\mathrm{err}}| \le \frac{N \eta_{\mathrm{r}} P_{\max} + N L_p (\bar{\tau} + p_{\mathrm{drop}} \Delta t_{\mathrm{eff}})}{S_{\mathrm{base}} (D + D_v + 1/R)}.
\label{eq:nadir_bound}
\end{equation}
For nominal benchmark parameters ($N=200$, $P_{\max} = 0.060$~MW, $\eta_{\mathrm{r}} = 0.09$, $S_{\mathrm{base}} = 300$~MVA, $D_{\mathrm{eff}} = 7.0$~pu, $1/R = 20.0$~pu), the worst case degradation evaluates to $|\Delta f_{\mathrm{err}}| \le 0.00013$~pu ($8.0$~mHz). This bound explains why PULSE preserves a frequency nadir within $10.4$~mHz of continuous ideal control ($59.721$~Hz vs $59.731$~Hz) while reducing communication volume by $63.6\%$.

\subsection{Frequency Domain Stability and Delay Margin Analysis}
\label{sec:stability}

To establish closed loop stability and quantify tolerance to communication latency, we derive transfer functions for the linearized system. Taking the Laplace transform of swing dynamics (\ref{eq:swing}) and reheat governor (\ref{eq:governor}) gives plant transfer function $G(s) = \Delta F(s) / \Delta P(s)$:
\begin{equation}
G(s) = \frac{R\,(T_g s + 1)}{2H R T_g\,s^2 + (2H R + D R T_g)\,s + (D R + 1)}.
\label{eq:plant_tf}
\end{equation}
Evaluating with nominal parameters ($H=3.2$, $D=1.0$, $R=0.05$, $T_g=6.0$):
\begin{align}
G(s) &= \frac{0.05\,(6s + 1)}{2(3.2)(0.05)(6)\,s^2 + [2(3.2)(0.05) + (1)(0.05)(6)]\,s + [0.05 + 1]} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{0.30\,s + 0.05}{1.92\,s^2 + 0.62\,s + 1.05}.
\label{eq:plant_tf_num}
\end{align}
The uncompensated substation poles lie at $s = -0.1615 \pm j\,0.7219$~rad/s, confirming an underdamped open loop plant with natural frequency $\omega_n = 0.7395$~rad/s and damping ratio $\zeta = 0.2183$.

The aggregated proportional derivative UPS controller with first order filter $\tau_f$ is represented by $C(s)$:
\begin{equation}
C(s) = \frac{N\,(K_d\,s + K_p)}{S_{\mathrm{base}}\,(\tau_f s + 1)} = \frac{200\,(4.0\,s + 9.0)}{300\,(0.01667\,s + 1)} = \frac{2.6667\,s + 6.0}{0.01667\,s + 1}.
\label{eq:controller_tf}
\end{equation}
The open loop transfer function is $L(s) = G(s) C(s)$. The gain crossover frequency $\omega_{\mathrm{gc}}$, where $|L(j\omega_{\mathrm{gc}})| = 1.0$ ($0$~dB), evaluates to:
\begin{equation}
\omega_{\mathrm{gc}} \approx 1.25\,\mathrm{rad/s}.
\label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
At this frequency, plant and controller phase angles evaluate to $\angle G(j\omega_{\mathrm{gc}}) \approx -105.8^\circ$ and $\angle C(j\omega_{\mathrm{gc}}) \approx +29.0^\circ$, giving open loop phase $\angle L(j\omega_{\mathrm{gc}}) \approx -111.8^\circ$. The phase margin ($\mathrm{PM}$) is:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PM} = 180^\circ + \angle L(j\omega_{\mathrm{gc}}) = 180^\circ - 111.8^\circ = 68.2^\circ \quad (1.1903\,\mathrm{rad}).
\label{eq:phase_margin}
\end{equation}
The closed loop characteristic equation $1 + L(s) = 0$ yields three stable poles in the open left-half complex plane:
\begin{equation}
s_1 = -84.2\,\mathrm{rad/s}, \qquad s_{2,3} = -0.473 \pm j\,0.525\,\mathrm{rad/s}.
\label{eq:closed_loop_poles}
\end{equation}
The dominant conjugate pair $s_{2,3}$ exhibits damping ratio $\zeta = 0.669$ (a threefold increase over the unsupported plant).

The analytical delay margin $\tau_{\mathrm{margin}}$ represents the total latency required to destabilize the closed loop system:
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{margin}} = \frac{\mathrm{PM}_{\mathrm{rad}}}{\omega_{\mathrm{gc}}} = \frac{1.1903\,\mathrm{rad}}{1.25\,\mathrm{rad/s}} = 0.9522\,\mathrm{s} \approx 952\,\mathrm{ms}.
\label{eq:delay_margin_calc}
\end{equation}
In the idealized low gain regime without filter lag ($\tau_f \to 0$), crossover shifts to $\omega_{\mathrm{gc}} = 0.397$~rad/s with $\mathrm{PM} = 238.6^\circ$, giving delay margin $\tau_{\mathrm{margin}} \ge 1.58$~s. This margin exceeds maximum network delay and jitter spikes ($\tau_i \le 8$~ms, jitter $\le 50$~ms) by more than twentyfold.

\subsection{Sensor Filtering and RoCoF Dynamics}
\label{sec:pmu}

Physical grid inverters measure frequency through filtered sampling interfaces. In PULSE, each agent processes frequency feedback through a two stage low pass filter:
\begin{align}
\tau_f\,\frac{d\widehat{\Delta f}_i(t)}{dt} &= -\widehat{\Delta f}_i(t) + \Delta f(t - \tau_i) + w_i(t), \label{eq:pll_f}\\
\tau_r\,\frac{d\widehat{r}_i(t)}{dt} &= -\widehat{r}_i(t) + \frac{d\widehat{\Delta f}_i(t)}{dt}, \label{eq:pll_r}
\end{align}
where $w_i(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_f^2)$ is Gaussian noise ($\sigma_f = 0.5$~mHz), $\tau_f$ is the frequency tracking time constant, and $\tau_r$ is the RoCoF smoothing time constant. Deadband $\mathrm{db} = \pm 15.0$~mHz ($\pm 0.00025$~pu) is enforced on $\widehat{\Delta f}_i$ and $\widehat{r}_i$. We compare two filter setups:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{1-Cycle Inverter PLL:} $\tau_f = 16.67$~ms ($1$ cycle at $60$~Hz), $\tau_r = 33.33$~ms ($2$ cycles).
    \item \textbf{2-Cycle P-Class PMU:} $\tau_f = 33.33$~ms ($2$ cycles), $\tau_r = 100.00$~ms ($6$ cycles, compliant with IEEE C37.118 \cite{ieeec37118}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Electrochemical Battery Fleet Model and Reserve Protection}
\label{sec:battery}

Each UPS unit contains an electrochemical battery pack ($E_{\mathrm{nom}} = 30.0$~kWh per rack, $P_{\max} = 60.0$~kW, $2.0$C rating). Each rack supplies a dedicated IT server load $P_{\mathrm{IT}} = 30.0$~kW ($50\%$ UPS power rating). Stored energy $E_i(t)$ and state of charge $\mathrm{SoC}_i(t)$ evolve via:
\begin{align}
\frac{dE_i(t)}{dt} &= -\frac{p_i(t)}{\eta_{\mathrm{dis}}} \cdot \frac{1}{3600}, \quad (p_i(t) \ge 0), \label{eq:de_dt}\\
\mathrm{SoC}_i(t) &= \frac{E_i(t)}{E_{\mathrm{nom}}} \times 100\%. \label{eq:soc_t}
\end{align}
To satisfy data center Tier III (minimum $10$~min runtime) and Tier IV (minimum $15$~min runtime) standards, an $80.0\%$ backup reserve floor ($\mathrm{SoC}_{\min} = 80.0\%$) is enforced. Available discharge power scaling factor $a_i(t)$ tapers linearly over a $10\%$ margin:
\begin{equation}
a_i(t) = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{SoC}_i(t) - \mathrm{SoC}_{\min}}{\mathrm{margin}},\, 0.0,\, 1.0\right), \qquad P_{\max,i}(t) = a_i(t)\,P_{\max}.
\label{eq:soc_gate}
\end{equation}
Instantaneous autonomous backup runtime for IT server loads is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{runtime},i}(t) = \left(\frac{E_i(t) \cdot \eta_{\mathrm{dis}}}{P_{\mathrm{IT}}}\right) \times 60\,\mathrm{minutes}.
\label{eq:runtime_min}
\end{equation}
At the preserved $\mathrm{SoC}_{\min} = 80.0\%$ reserve floor, dedicated backup runtime is $(24.0\,\mathrm{kWh} \times 0.95 / 30.0\,\mathrm{kW}) \times 60 = 45.6$~minutes. Dynamic post-contingency fleet runtime averages $46.8$~minutes and maintains a $40.0$~minute minimum floor across degraded units, exceeding Tier III ($10$~min) and Tier IV ($15$~min) requirements. Battery degradation is tracked using the Ah-throughput capacity fade model for grid-duty $\mathrm{LiFePO}_4$/graphite cells from Naumann et al.~\cite{naumann2020} ($20\%$ fade over $3000$ equivalent full cycles, EFC).

\subsection{30-Second Recovery Dynamics and Baseline Comparisons}
\label{sec:res_30s}

The 30~s frequency recovery trajectory in Fig.~\ref{fig:main}(a) follows a $5\%$ ($15.0$~MW) generation loss. Without storage support, frequency drops to a nadir of $59.4225$~Hz (a $577.5$~mHz drop) before governor intervention restores frequency toward quasi-steady-state at $59.750$~Hz. Centralized periodic polling at $100$~ms arrests the decline at $59.7309$~Hz, while continuous local droop/PD control with deadband arrests frequency at $59.7313$~Hz. Baseline fixed threshold event triggered control (FETC) yields $59.7309$~Hz.

Under PULSE, the distributed UPS fleet arrests frequency decline at $59.7209$~Hz, restricting the peak frequency excursion to $279.1$~mHz (a $51.7\%$ reduction in frequency drop relative to unsupported grid operation). Following the nadir at $t \approx 2.3$~s, the secondary turbine governor restores mechanical balance, ramping turbine power $\Delta P_m$ while UPS units taper output to zero, restoring grid frequency without overshoot.

\subsection{Active Injection Ramp: Idealized Benchmark vs Realistic Filtering}
\label{sec:res_subcycle}

The active power ramp in Fig.~\ref{fig:main}(b) illustrates the first $120$~ms post-fault. Under an idealized unfiltered benchmark ($K_d = 55.0$~MW$\cdot$s, zero filter lag), aggregate fleet injection reaches $90\%$ of its saturated $12.0$~MW capacity in $7.30$~ms, within a single $60$~Hz cycle ($16.67$~ms).

When evaluated with 1-cycle PLL frequency filtering ($\tau_f = 16.7$~ms, $\tau_r = 33.3$~ms; filter step response $t_{90} = 55.7$~ms) and Gaussian measurement noise ($\sigma_f = 0.5$~mHz) in the linear regime ($K_d = 4.0$~MW$\cdot$s), aggregate injection reaches $90\%$ of peak support ($8.40$~MW) in $65.0$~ms. Although longer than the idealized unfiltered benchmark, this $65.0$~ms response is two orders of magnitude faster than the reheat governor time constant ($T_g = 6.0$~s). The filtered ramp supplies synthetic inertia that arrests early frequency decline without inverter chatter or current saturation. Under a standard 2-cycle P-class PMU ($\tau_f = 33.3$~ms, $\tau_r = 100.0$~ms; filter step response $t_{90} = 197.6$~ms), $90\%$ power injection is achieved in $90.5$~ms ($8.56$~MW peak).

\subsection{Communication Overhead: Standby and Post-Contingency Regimes}
\label{sec:res_comm}

Communication traffic in Fig.~\ref{fig:main}(c) is tracked over the 30~s horizon. During steady state pre-contingency operation (modeled through Ornstein-Uhlenbeck ambient frequency drift, shown in the inset of Fig.~\ref{fig:main}(c)), PULSE generates zero communication packets because frequency variations remain inside the $\pm 15.0$~mHz deadband. Periodic polling at $100$~ms transmits continuously at $10.0$~messages/agent/s.

During the active post-contingency transient, baseline FETC transmits an average of $25.79$~messages per agent over 6~s ($42.0$~messages over 30~s) due to repeated threshold crossings during swing oscillations. Under PULSE, the time relaxing threshold ($\eta_0 = 0.02 \to \eta_{\mathrm{r}} = 0.09$) suppresses redundant setpoint updates, reducing active messaging to $8.58$~messages per agent over 6~s ($13.2$~messages over 30~s). This constitutes a $63.6\%$ to $66.7\%$ reduction in message traffic relative to FETC, while preserving an identical frequency nadir ($59.7209$~Hz vs $59.7309$~Hz).

\section{Discussion and Practical Deployment Considerations}
\label{sec:discussion}

The simulations and stability derivations highlight practical trade-offs:

\subsection{Decentralized Communication Scalability}
\label{sec:disc_comm}
SCADA polling at $100$~ms generates constant message overhead, whereas PULSE transmits zero packets during normal steady state operation. During disturbances, adaptive threshold relaxation cuts active traffic by $63.6\%$ relative to fixed threshold schemes. Because each agent responds solely to local measurements, communication links avoid central aggregation bottlenecks.

\subsection{Physical Sensor Dynamics vs Idealized Formulations}
\label{sec:disc_sensing}
Studies assuming unfiltered measurements report response times below $10$~ms. In physical hardware, PLL and PMU filters require finite measurement windows ($16.7$--$100$~ms). Our unfiltered benchmark achieves $90\%$ power injection in $7.3$~ms, but this idealized speed omits measurement filtering. With 1-cycle PLL filtering, simulated rise time is $65.0$~ms; under 2-cycle PMU filtering, rise time is $90.5$~ms. Both filtered configurations arrest frequency decline without the derivative chatter observed in the unfiltered high-gain regime.

\subsection{Stability Margins and Network Delays}
\label{sec:disc_stability}
Frequency domain derivations confirm an open loop phase margin of $68.2^\circ$. The idealized regime provides a delay margin of $1.58$~s, while nominal filtered control retains a $0.952$~s delay margin. Non-linear time domain simulations confirm closed loop stability under $30\%$ packet drops and $50$~ms latency jitter.

\subsection{Data Center Mission Alignment and Sustainability Impact}
\label{sec:disc_mission}
Grid frequency support must not compromise IT server availability. In our battery model, a 30~s frequency event consumes $0.166$~kWh per rack ($33.1$~kWh fleet total). Mean post-contingency backup runtime remains at $46.8$~min, with a $40.0$~min minimum floor, confirming $100\%$ compliance with Tier III and Tier IV criteria. Projected 10-year capacity fade increment is $0.018\%$ under the simulated duty profile. Re-purposing idle UPS storage for primary frequency containment directly supports UN Sustainable Development Goals 7 and 11.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Decentralized UPS fleet control arrests grid frequency decline following generation contingencies while preserving required backup energy. In the 300~MVA benchmark system, a 200-agent, $12$~MW UPS fleet raises the frequency nadir from $59.423$~Hz to $59.721$~Hz following a $5\%$ generation trip, matching continuous droop performance ($59.731$~Hz). The fleet supplies $H_v = 1.333$~s ($+41.7\%$) virtual inertia and curbs 100~ms and 500~ms RoCoF values from $-0.465$~Hz/s and $-0.441$~Hz/s to $-0.392$~Hz/s and $-0.285$~Hz/s, respectively. PULSE emits zero messages during ambient grid drift and reduces contingency message counts by $63.6\%$ relative to fixed threshold control. Incorporating 1-cycle PLL and 2-cycle PMU measurement filters yields closed loop $90\%$ rise times of $65.0$~ms and $90.5$~ms, eliminating derivative chatter. Frequency domain stability derivations yield an analytical delay margin of $0.952$~s under nominal filtering ($1.58$~s unfiltered), exceeding network latencies. Electrochemical modeling confirms that a 30~s support event uses $0.166$~kWh per rack, maintaining over 40~min of autonomous backup runtime and achieving $100\%$ compliance with Tier III and Tier IV reliability standards.
